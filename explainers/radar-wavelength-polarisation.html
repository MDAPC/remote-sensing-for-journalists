Radar brings its own light

Optical satellites need sunlight. Radar satellites send their own microwave pulses and listen for the echo, day or night, through clouds and smoke. Two choices shape what radar sees: how long its waves are, and which way they shake.

Wavelength decides how deep radar sees

A wave mostly ignores things much smaller than itself and bounces off things its own size or larger. Short waves stop at the leaves. Long waves slip past them to branches, trunks and even into dry sand. Choose a radar band.

Polarisation: which way the wave shakes

Shake a rope up and down and the wave is vertical. Shake it side to side and it is horizontal. A radar chooses the direction it sends, and listens in one or both directions. Sentinel-1 sends vertical over land and listens to both, giving the two images you see in every Sentinel-1 product: VV and VH.

VVertical: shakes up and down

HHorizontal: shakes side to side

What comes back depends on what it hits

The satellite sends a vertical wave. VV is the echo that comes back still vertical. VH is the echo that comes back turned sideways, which only happens when the wave bounces around inside something messy, like a tree crown. Pick a target.

What to remember

Dark means smooth or empty

Calm water and smooth tarmac bounce the pulse away like a mirror. Very dark patches on land can reveal floods.

Bright means corners

Buildings form corners with the ground that throw the pulse straight back. When a building is destroyed, the bright spot fades.

VH is a vegetation detector

Only messy, three-dimensional targets like tree crowns twist the wave. Compare VV and VH to tell crops and forest from buildings.

Free radar is C-band

Sentinel-1 is free and global. Longer bands from ALOS-2, NISAR or BIOMASS see deeper but data access varies.

Penetration depths and echo strengths are simplified for teaching. Real values depend on incidence angle, moisture and surface details.
